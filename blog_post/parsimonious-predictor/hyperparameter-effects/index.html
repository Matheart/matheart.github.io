Hyperparameter effects against the literatureThe one-knob effects and two-knob maps of the Marin and StepLaw sweeps, checked against the source papers, independent work and the runs themselves

Companion to
A Parsimonious Predictor of Pre-training Dynamics
Sets of runs
Marin optimizer study, StepLaw dense runs
Published
November 24, 2026

Our post on predicting pre-training dynamics showed two kinds of hyperparameter effect. The first kind is a one-knob effect, read inside a stratum: a set of measured runs in one cell (one model size and token budget, and for Marin one optimizer) that differ in one knob only, so no model is involved. The second kind is a two-knob map from a pairwise spline model fitted to the measured final losses. Each map is a partial-dependence plot: the model's prediction for two inputs, averaged over all other inputs. Here we check each effect against the paper that produced the runs, against independent work, and, for the maps, against the same quantity measured directly from the runs.

The runs come from two public sweeps. Marin is the optimizer study of Wen et al.[1]: 1,560 runs of 13 optimizers at 134M to 1.2B parameters, tuned by changing one knob at a time around the best configuration. StepLaw is the learning-rate and batch-size grid of Li et al.[2], of which we use the 1,631 dense runs.

Which results hold up

Most one-knob effects are not new. Six of the nine repeat the tuning results of Wen et al., the paper that produced the Marin runs, and agree with independent work. Two may be new. The range of near-best learning rates widens as the model grows, which no study we found measures. A decay over 80% of training or more beats a 22% decay by a median 0.05 nats, which does not fit the report that a cooldown of about 20% is enough.

The two-knob maps hold up less well. Measured directly from the StepLaw runs, three of the four map slopes that we could check change. The best batch grows about as tokens0.2, not tokens0.9. The best learning rate rises by about a third of a decade per decade of batch, not two thirds. At a fixed batch, the best learning rate does not change with tokens. The maps average over cells whose optima differ, and that average moves the slopes. The post used these slopes to reconcile the opposite learning-rate trends of Bjorck et al. and the StepLaw paper, and the runs do not support that reconciliation.

One knob at a time

Each plot shows one line per stratum: the measured final losses of the runs that differ only in this knob, as the loss above the best run of the line. Blue lines are model sizes up to 430M parameters and orange lines are larger, held-out sizes. The knob was changed one at a time from a configuration tuned at the other values, so a value that needs the others retuned may look worse than it is.

Learning rate, StepLaw

No prior result found

Inside each of the 34 StepLaw cells, at the cell's best batch, the final loss is a single bowl in the log learning rate. The range of learning rates within 0.01 nats of the best run widens from about 0.3 to 0.55 decades as the model grows. Model size and tokens per parameter rise together in this sweep, so the two causes cannot be separated here. In our transfer test on the runs of Porian et al.[8] at a fixed 20 tokens per parameter, the range also widens, from 0.20 to 0.68 decades between 5M and 221M parameters.

Li et al.[2] describe the landscape as convex with a broad optimum (Section 3.3, Figure 4), but give no width and no trend with size. Wortsman et al.[3] report that their learning-rate sensitivity grows with scale (Figure 1). Their measure averages the loss over a fixed range from 3e-4 to 3e-1 and is dominated by divergence at the high end, so it measures something different from the near-best width. We found no measurement of the near-best width against model size.

Decay length, Marin

Partly agrees

The best decay covers 80% of training or more in 29 of 33 cells. In the 16 cells that also tried a decay over 22% of training, that decay is worse than the best by a median 0.05 nats, from 0.02 to 0.11. The decay strata come from eight optimizers, none of them AdamW.

Wen et al.[1] tuned the decay length as one of their knobs but report no separate result about it. Bergsma et al.[4] find that decaying linearly to zero over the whole run beats cosine decay to 10% of the peak, and beats a schedule with a 22.5% cooldown (Figure 8). Hägele et al.[5] report that the gain from a longer cooldown levels off near 20% of training (Section 3.2, Figure 5), and MiniCPM[6] uses about 10%. A gap of 0.05 nats at 22% fits Bergsma et al. and does not fit a plateau at 20%, although Hägele et al. used AdamW, which these cells do not include.

Adam beta2, Marin

Agrees

Across 0.9 to 0.995, beta2 moves the final loss by about 0.02 nats in a typical cell. 0.99 is best in 15 of 30 cells and 0.98 in 8.

These runs are part of the tuning grid of Wen et al.[1], so the result repeats their own tables. Zhang et al.[7] and Porian et al.[8] find that a large beta2, 0.99 or more, matters at small batch sizes. Marek et al.[9] keep the half-life of the second-moment average fixed in tokens, so the best beta2 depends on the batch size. Zhao et al.[10] find effects of up to about 0.02 in validation loss.

Adam epsilon, Marin

Agrees

From 1e-25 to 1e-7, epsilon moves the final loss by about 0.011 nats in a typical cell, and no value is best in most cells.

These runs are part of the tuning grid of Wen et al.[1]. Wortsman et al.[3] (Section 3.4) and Everett et al.[11] (Section 4.3) show that epsilon matters when the root-mean-square gradient falls to its size, which happens in larger models. At 4.8B parameters, Wortsman et al. report divergence at 1e-6 and a gain at 1e-15. A flat effect at 134M to 1.2B parameters fits this, although we did not measure the gradient size in these runs.

Gradient clipping, Marin

No prior result found

Clipping the gradient norm at 1, at 2 or not at all moves the final loss by about 0.007 nats in a typical cell. Only 8 cells vary it, and clipping at 1 is best in 7 of them.

These runs are part of the tuning grid of Wen et al.[1]. We found no independent study that measures the final-loss effect of a fixed clipping threshold in language-model pretraining. Papers on adaptive clipping compare against a fixed threshold of 1, but we did not read their final-loss numbers.

Adam beta1, Marin

Agrees

Across 0.8 to 0.99, beta1 moves the final loss by about 0.03 nats, and the best value differs from cell to cell: 0.95 in 10 of 33 cells, 0.98 in 9, 0.9 in 7 and 0.8 in 6.

Wen et al.[1] list beta1 as sensitive to scale only for Mars and Scion (Table 4). Zhao et al.[10] find Adam robust from about 0.8 to 0.98 (Section 2.4). Orvieto and Gower[12] find that a beta1 equal to beta2 is close to optimal (Section 3.1), so the best beta1 depends on beta2, which may explain the variation between cells.

Weight decay, Marin

Agrees

Weight decay moves the final loss by about 0.03 nats, and the best value ranges from 0.1 to 0.9 depending on the cell.

Wen et al.[1] report a best weight decay near 0.1 for AdamW and near 0.6 for Lion (Figure 1, Section 4.2), so most of the spread is between optimizers. Wang and Aitchison[13] argue that the timescale 1/(learning rate × weight decay) should stay roughly fixed, so the best weight decay moves with the learning rate and the data size.

Learning rate, Marin

Agrees

Inside a cell, the learning rate moves the final loss by about 0.03 nats across the tested values, and the best value depends on the optimizer. An average over optimizers, as in a partial-dependence plot, mixes these different curves.

This repeats Wen et al.[1], for whom the learning rate is the most sensitive knob, with a different best value for each optimizer (Figure 1). Zhao et al.[10] find the best learning rate of Lion about ten times smaller than that of Adam.

Batch size at fixed tokens, Marin

Agrees

At a fixed token budget, batch size has the largest one-knob effect, about 0.12 nats in a typical cell. The smallest tested batch, 0.52M tokens, is best in 9 of 11 cells.

The best batch that Li et al.[2] fit, 0.58 D0.571 tokens, is 0.14M to 0.46M tokens at the token budgets of these cells, below the smallest batch that Marin tried. The two exceptions are the cells with 21.5B tokens, where the fitted best batch, 0.46M, is close to the smallest tested one. Marek et al.[9] also find the smallest batch best in small models, and Porian et al.[8] find a floor below which a smaller batch stops helping.

Two knobs at a time

Each map shows the pairwise spline model's final loss above the map's minimum, averaged over all other inputs. The white line is the map's lowest-loss value of the horizontal input for each value of the vertical one, and white circles are training runs. Red diamonds are measured best values, read from the runs without a model, for the training sizes that the map was fitted on. Where the white line and the diamonds part, the map's slope comes from averaging, not from the runs.

Marin: learning rate by weight decay

Partly agrees

The map's lowest-loss line falls with a slope close to −1 on log axes, so the product of learning rate and weight decay is about constant along it. The Marin runs change one knob at a time around a tuned configuration, so no cell varies both knobs. The slope comes from the model pooling cells and optimizers, and optimizers with a smaller best learning rate also use a larger weight decay. The diamonds are the 8 cells with a measured best value of both. Their product spans about a decade across optimizers and a factor of two within SOAP.

Fan et al.[14] vary both knobs on one grid and find a diagonal ridge of near-optimal settings: a larger weight decay needs a smaller learning rate (Figure 4). They do not fit a slope. Wang and Aitchison[13] predict a fixed product at a fixed number of steps, and Bergsma et al.[15] find that the best timescale, batch / (learning rate × weight decay × tokens), changes with tokens per parameter.

Marin: learning rate by tokens

Agrees

The map's best learning rate falls as tokens grow. Measured directly, within one optimizer and model size, the best learning rate falls with tokens in three of four groups, with a median slope of −0.2 per decade of tokens. The diamonds are the measured best learning rates of the learning-rate strata.

Bjorck et al.[16] fit a best learning rate proportional to D−0.32 at a fixed batch of 0.5M tokens, and DeepSeek LLM[17] fits a best learning rate that falls with compute. Filatov et al.[18] find the best learning rate falling with tokens at a small fixed batch.

Marin: warmup fraction by tokens

No prior result found

The map shows no clear trend in the best warmup fraction with tokens. Few warmup values were run, so the map rests on sparse data, and its high best value is an extrapolation between the runs.

Wen et al.[1] list warmup as sensitive to scale for several optimizers without fitting a trend. Porian et al.[8] tie the warmup length to model size, with about as many warmup tokens as parameters. We found no measurement of the best warmup fraction against tokens.

StepLaw: learning rate by batch

Partly agrees

The map's best learning rate rises by about two thirds of a decade per decade of batch. Measured directly inside each cell, it rises by a median 0.34 decade per decade, with quartiles 0.23 and 0.49 over 31 cells. The diamonds are the measured best learning rate of each cell and batch. The map's slope comes from partial dependence, which averages over cells whose optima differ.

The square-root rule for Adam[19] predicts 0.5, and Li et al.[20] find that the best learning rate rises below a noise scale and falls above it. Bergsma et al.[15] report that the best learning rate changes little with batch below the critical batch size when the weight decay is tuned instead. The measured 0.34 lies between 0 and 0.5. The StepLaw paper[2] gives no trend for this pair.

StepLaw: learning rate by model size

Agrees

The map's best learning rate falls with model size with a slope of about −0.6 on log axes. Measured directly at each cell's best batch, a fit of the log best learning rate on log model size and log tokens gives −0.64 for model size.

Li et al.[2] fit −0.713, and Porian et al.[8] also find the best learning rate falling with model size.

StepLaw: learning rate by tokens

Conflicts

The map's best learning rate falls slightly as tokens grow. Measured directly at a fixed model size and batch, it does not change: the median slope is 0.00 per decade of tokens over 36 groups, with quartiles −0.13 and 0.21. At each cell's best batch the slope is +0.08.

Bjorck et al.[16] fit −0.32 at a fixed batch, and Li et al.[2] fit +0.307 along the best batch. Neither value appears in these runs, which are the dense subset of the StepLaw release, not all the runs that the paper fitted. The measured slopes agree with each other: a best batch growing as tokens0.2 and a best learning rate growing as batch0.34 give about +0.07, close to the measured +0.08. The post's agreement with +0.307 came from the map's slopes. Filatov et al.[18] find that the sign of this trend depends on the batch relative to the critical batch size.

StepLaw: best batch by tokens

Partly agrees

The map's best batch grows with tokens with a slope of about 0.9 on log axes. Measured directly, the best batch of each cell, from a parabola through the three batches around the minimum, grows with a slope of 0.18 over 31 cells, or 0.28 without the 2 cells whose best batch is at the edge of the tested range. The diamonds are these measured best batches. Each cell tests only a few batches, so the measured slope is a rough estimate.

Li et al.[2] fit 0.571. Bergsma et al.[15] find a best batch growing as about tokens0.4, Zhang et al.[7] fit a critical batch growing as tokens0.47, and Filatov et al.[18] find a best batch growing as about the square root of the training length. All agree on the direction. The map's 0.9 is above every published value, and the measured 0.2 to 0.3 is below them.

StepLaw: relative learning rate by relative batch

No prior result found

The map shows the best learning rate at its reference value for large batches and below it for small batches. The reference values come from the best runs at each model size. This follows from the learning-rate-by-batch result above, and we did not measure it separately.

Li et al.[20] find that the best learning rate rises with batch below a noise scale and levels off near it, which fits this map. We found no study that shows the same normalized view.

How the literature was searched

Two search agents read the source paper and independent work for each panel, with about 55 web searches and page reads in all, mostly of the HTML versions on arXiv. Section, figure and table numbers refer to those versions. The appendix tables of Wen et al. were read only in part. "No prior result found" means that this search found none, not that none exists. The measured values come from the same runs as the post's figures.
